How to name a business: the complete guide for founders

10 min readFounders, entrepreneurs, freelancersUpdated 2026

Your business name will follow you for years — appearing on invoices, in pitches, in search results, and in every introduction you make. Getting it right is worth the effort. This guide covers everything from initial brainstorming to final checks, with practical frameworks used by professional brand strategists.

What makes a great business name?

The qualities of an effective business name have remained remarkably consistent across industries and decades. The companies whose names have endured — Apple, Google, Amazon, Canva, Slack — share a set of characteristics that are replicable and learnable.

Short. The most memorable brand names are typically one or two syllables. Apple. Slack. Zoom. Nike. Short names are easier to remember, easier to say, easier to type, and easier to use as a hashtag or handle. Beyond four syllables, names become unwieldy in everyday use.

Easy to spell and pronounce. If people can't spell your name, they can't find you online. If they can't pronounce it, they won't say it — and word-of-mouth is still the most powerful marketing available. Never make customers feel uncertain about how to say your name.

Available as a domain. In 2026, your .com domain is your address. A business name without an available .com (or at minimum a relevant TLD like .co or .io) creates lasting friction. Check domain availability before falling in love with any name.

Distinctive and ownable. Your name should stand out in your category. If your industry is full of names beginning with "Pro" or ending in "-ify", standing apart from that pattern gives you an instant advantage in memorability.

Legally clear. A name that's too similar to an existing trademark is a liability. Always run trademark searches before committing.

The six types of business names

Understanding the different structural approaches to business naming helps you make a deliberate choice rather than defaulting to whatever sounds good in the moment.

Descriptive
Directly describes what you do. Easy to understand, hard to trademark strongly, and can limit future expansion.
e.g. General Electric, British Airways, The Financial Times
Invented / coined
Made-up words with no prior meaning. Highly trademarkable, requires brand building to create association.
e.g. Kodak, Xerox, Häagen-Dazs, Rolex
Evocative
Suggests qualities or feelings without literally describing the product. The sweet spot for most modern brands.
e.g. Apple, Amazon, Virgin, Innocent, Patagonia
Acronym
Initials of a longer name. Familiar when established, anonymous and hard to remember when new.
e.g. IBM, BMW, HSBC — all built on decades of marketing spend
Founder name
Uses the founder's name. Strong personal brand, can complicate sales or succession. Works best in professional services.
e.g. Goldman Sachs, Ford, Dyson, Burberry
Compound / blend
Combines two meaningful words or parts. Creates new meaning from familiar parts. Very popular in tech.
e.g. Facebook, Snapchat, Pinterest, YouTube, Dropbox

For most early-stage founders, the evocative and compound approaches offer the best balance of memorability, trademarkability, and domain availability. Purely descriptive names are difficult to trademark; purely invented names require significant marketing investment to build meaning.

Brainstorming techniques that actually work

Start with what you want people to feel

Before you think about names, think about emotions and associations. When a customer uses your product or service, what do you want them to feel? Write down 10-20 words that capture those feelings. These become your naming territory — the semantic field from which strong names can be drawn.

A legal services firm might generate: clarity, confidence, protection, precision, resolution, solid, clear, strong, trusted. A children's education platform might generate: discovery, wonder, curiosity, adventure, bright, spark, grow, explore, play.

Names that genuinely evoke these feelings don't necessarily contain the words themselves — Amazon evokes scale and depth without saying "big" or "lots." But understanding your target emotions guides evaluation of candidate names.

Use linguistic techniques

Some of the most effective brand names were generated through deliberate linguistic manipulation rather than random brainstorming. Techniques worth exploring include: alliteration (PayPal, Coca-Cola, Krispy Kreme); onomatopoeia (Snap, Zip, Buzz); misspelling (Tumblr, Flickr, Fiverr); portmanteau (Pinterest = pin + interest; Instagram = instant + telegram); foreign language (Volvo = "I roll" in Latin; Reebok = a type of antelope); and metaphor (Amazon, Apple, Blackberry).

Mine unexpected territory

Some of the most distinctive names come from borrowing from completely unrelated domains. A fintech startup named after a geological feature (Monzo). A streaming service named after a historical figure (Spotify is not named after a person, but the category of culturally unexpected names is rich). An accountancy firm named after a constellation. The more unexpected the source, the more memorable and ownable the name.

The four tests every business name must pass

Business name validation checklist
🔍
The phone test: Say the name aloud, then spell it out. Would someone on the phone be able to spell it correctly without you having to repeat yourself? If you find yourself saying "V for Victor, E, L, O" rather than just "Velo", it may cause ongoing friction.
🌐
The domain test: Check .com availability on Namecheap, Porkbun, or a similar registrar. Also check .co.uk if you're UK-focused, and .io if you're in tech. If the .com is taken, research who owns it and whether it's actively used — parked domains are sometimes purchasable.
™️
The trademark test: Search the UK Intellectual Property Office (gov.uk/search-for-trademark) and the EU trademark database (euipo.europa.eu) for similar marks in your category. Also do a general Google search — an existing brand with a similar name in your industry is a problem even without a registered trademark.
🌍
The translation test: If you expect to operate internationally, check your name doesn't have unfortunate meanings in other languages. Several major brands have learned this lesson expensively — the Chevrolet Nova ("no va" = "doesn't go" in Spanish) is the famous example, though the impact is often overstated.
📱
The social media test: Check handle availability on Instagram, LinkedIn, X/Twitter, and TikTok. Consistent handles across platforms are a meaningful advantage for brand building. If your name is taken everywhere, it signals either that you need a modifier or a different name.

Common naming mistakes to avoid

Limiting geography unnecessarily. "Yorkshire Digital" or "Bristol Accounting" locks you to a location you may grow beyond. Unless locality is a deliberate brand choice, avoid geographical specifics in your core name.

Too clever. Names that require explanation, involve wordplay only some people will get, or reference niche cultural knowledge create barriers. The name should work for everyone in your target market, not just the most culturally informed.

Trendy spellings. Dropping vowels (Tumblr, Flickr) was a tech naming convention of the 2010s that now reads as dated. Deliberate misspellings for domain availability (Kustom, Kwik) can undermine professional credibility in contexts where spelling competence is expected.

Generic plus industry. "ProTech Solutions", "SmartBiz Consulting", "Digital Marketing Plus" — these names are unmemorable precisely because they describe the category without distinguishing themselves within it. Generic compound names are almost impossible to trademark and virtually impossible to rank for in search.

Ignoring the logo test. Before finalising a name, sketch how it might look as a logo. Long names are difficult to render as icons or marks. Single-word names with distinctive visual qualities (Apple, Shell, Target) have a significant advantage in visual brand building.

The founder bias problem: Founders almost always have strong emotional attachments to certain name candidates — often names they thought of first, or names with personal meaning. Getting feedback from people outside your company and outside your industry is essential. They will tell you what the name actually communicates to someone with no prior context.

When to hire a professional naming consultant

Professional brand naming consultants charge anywhere from £2,000 to £50,000+ for a naming project, depending on scope and agency size. For most early-stage businesses, this is not a justified expense — the tools and techniques above are sufficient, and an imperfect name that you actually launch with beats a perfect name you never settle on.

Professional naming makes sense when: you're operating in a highly competitive category where differentiation is critical; you have significant budget for brand building and need IP protection from day one; you've failed to find a workable name after genuine effort; or you're renaming an established business where existing brand equity is at stake.

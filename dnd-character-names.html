D&D character names: a complete guide by class and race

11 min readD&D players, game mastersUpdated 2026

Naming your D&D character well takes more than picking something that sounds cool. The best names suggest your character's background, class, and personality — and hold up session after session at the table. This guide covers naming conventions for every major class and the most popular races in 5th edition.

Why your D&D character's name matters

In a tabletop RPG, your character's name will be spoken aloud dozens of times each session — by you, by your DM, and by your fellow players. It will appear in the DM's notes, in session recaps, and potentially in the memories of everyone at the table for years to come. A name that's hard to pronounce, awkward to say mid-sentence, or that clashes with the tone of the campaign will create low-level friction every time it comes up.

Beyond practicality, a well-chosen name contributes to roleplay. When your name suits your character's background and class, it becomes a small but constant signal about who they are. When a player introduces their character, the name alone can set expectations — for better or worse.

Naming by class

Class should inform your naming choice because class reflects background, training, and cultural context. A wizard typically comes from an educated background — their name might reflect scholarly or arcane tradition. A barbarian probably wasn't named at a formal naming ceremony with elaborate family traditions. Here's a practical guide to naming by class.

⚔️Fighter
Fighters can come from almost any background — noble knights, mercenaries, soldiers, gladiators. Names should feel solid and grounded. Short, strong syllables work well. Epithets earned in battle are common ("the Bold", "Ironwall", "Steelborn").
Aldric Ironwall Brennan Steelborn Mira Stormshield Harlan Stoneback Torvak the Bold
🔮Wizard
Wizards typically have educated, often multi-syllabic names reflecting scholarly or arcane tradition. Names ending in -us, -ix, -rix, or -ath suggest classical education. Many wizards adopt a title or suffix that reflects their specialisation.
Arcandus Veil Thessia Spellwright Zephyros the Knowing Caldric Runebind Nyxara the Pale
🗡️Rogue
Rogues often use aliases, street names, or pseudonyms alongside or instead of birth names. Epithets describing their method or reputation are common. Dark, slippery sounds work well — shadow, whisper, hollow, silver, night.
Shadow Jack Vesper Nightwhisper Cade the Quick Mira Silvershadow Lothar Lightfinger
⚜️Paladin
Paladins often have names that reflect their devotion — either through direct meaning (names associated with light, dawn, flame, justice) or through the formal "Ser/Sister/Brother" honorific. Noble, clear sounds suit the class.
Ser Aldric of Dawn Theron the Just Ser Elara Goldenshield Brynn Lightbringer Daric of the Flame
🌿Druid
Druids often take nature names — plants, animals, natural phenomena — either as birth names from communities close to nature, or as chosen names adopted upon joining a druidic circle. Celtic phonetic patterns work particularly well.
Ashwood Fern Briar Mosswhisper Wren Rootsong Eirne of the Glen Sylvan Thornwood
🎵Bard
Bards often have memorable, slightly theatrical names — easy to say, easy to remember, with a touch of flair. Some adopt stage names. Alliteration and rhythm suit the class. Names that sound good spoken aloud are a natural fit.
Pip the Golden Lyra Brightstring Caleb the Wanderer Lorcan Lightfingers Tam the Nimble
🌑Warlock
Warlock names should suggest their pact — something bound, named, tethered, or hollowed. The best warlock names carry a sense of transaction or cost. Dark sounds and references to shadow, void, or chains work well.
Vesper the Bound Draven Voidpact Thyrak the Named Noctis Greymantle Elara the Hollow
✝️Cleric
Clerics often carry religious titles (Brother, Sister, Father, Blessed) alongside their name, reflecting their role. Names with meanings associated with healing, light, devotion, or their specific deity's domain are appropriate.
Brother Aldric Sister Mira of Dawn Theron the Blessed Piety Graceborn Calla Dawnhymn

Naming by race

Elves

The D&D Player's Handbook provides extensive naming guidance for elves, distinguishing between child names (simple, used until adulthood), adult names (melodic and reflecting personality or deeds), and family names (ancient and tied to lineage). Elven names are characterised by soft consonants, open vowels, and often apostrophes marking linguistic stress points. High elves tend towards more formal, multi-syllabic names; wood elves towards shorter, nature-influenced names.

Dwarves

Dwarf names in D&D come in masculine and feminine variants with distinct sounds. Clan names are equally important — dwarves are intensely proud of lineage. Masculine names tend to be harder and shorter; feminine names often have similar qualities but with slightly softer endings. Using real Norse or Old Germanic names as inspiration is reliable and feels authentic to the tradition.

Halflings

Halfling names in D&D tend to be cheerful, simple, and often diminutive — reflecting their culture of hospitality and community. Family names often relate to professions, physical characteristics, or lucky events. Think English rural names with a slightly whimsical quality: Tosser Goodbarrel, Merric Leagallow, Cora Tealeaf.

Humans

Human names in D&D reflect the enormous cultural diversity of human societies in the setting. The Player's Handbook provides regional naming lists. The flexibility of human names means you have the widest range of choices — from obviously medieval European to Far Eastern, Middle Eastern, or African influenced traditions, depending on your character's cultural background.

Tieflings

Tieflings have two naming traditions — those raised in human society often have human names, while others choose names reflecting their infernal heritage or their own nature. The "virtue name" tradition — Hope, Despair, Glory, Revelation, Fear — creates memorable tiefling characters. Infernal-sounding names using harsh consonants and unusual vowel patterns are also common.

Practical tips for the table

The three-second test: Say your character's name aloud, quickly, three times. If it feels clunky, your DM and fellow players will find it clunky too. The best table names are easy to say at normal conversational speed, impossible to accidentally mispronounce in embarrassing ways, and distinct from every other character at the table.

Check your party first. Before settling on a name, find out what your fellow players are calling their characters. Having two characters whose names begin with the same letter, or which rhyme, will cause constant confusion. A quick check before session one saves a lot of "wait, who?" moments.

Consider a nickname. Many players find it useful to have both a full name and a common name. "Thessia Spellwright" might be how the character is formally known but "Tess" is what the party calls her at the table. This gives you the flavour of a well-developed name without requiring everyone to manage syllables mid-combat.

Leave room for the name to grow. The best D&D character names earn epithets through play — moments at the table that stick and become part of how the character is remembered. Starting with a simple name and letting "the Bold" or "Gravedigger" or "Twice-Damned" emerge organically is often more satisfying than pre-planning every element.
